### Day 1 · Why Short Reviews Help (111 words · B1)

Many students read a new word several times and feel certain that they know it. However, seeing a word is not the same as recalling it without help. A short quiz can reveal what the learner can actually remember. Reviewing the same material on different days also offers a useful challenge: the learner must bring the information back after some forgetting has occurred. This does not mean every review should be difficult. When a student makes a mistake, a clear explanation and another chance to answer can make the next attempt more useful. Over time, a series of short reviews may be easier to maintain than one very long study session.

**문항 1 — 글의 중심 생각으로 가장 적절한 것은?**

  - A. 긴 공부 한 번이면 복습은 필요 없다.
  - **B. 짧은 회상 연습과 간격을 둔 복습이 도움이 될 수 있다.**
  - C. 단어는 소리 내어 읽기만 하면 충분하다.
  - D. 오답을 피하기 위해 쉬운 문제만 풀어야 한다.
  - **정답**: B
  - **해설**: 단순히 읽는 것과 스스로 떠올리는 것이 다르며 여러 날에 걸쳐 짧게 복습하는 장점을 설명한다.
  - **근거**: `Reviewing the same material on different days also offers a useful challenge:`

**문항 2 — 글에서 단순히 단어를 여러 번 보는 것의 한계는?**

  - **해설**: 반복해서 보는 것과 도움 없이 떠올리는 것은 다르다고 설명한다.

### Day 2 · Five Minutes That Stayed (138 words · B1)

At the beginning of the semester, Hana made a plan to study English for one hour every evening. She followed it for two days, but soon other homework made the plan difficult. Instead of giving up, she changed the plan to five minutes of vocabulary review after breakfast. Five minutes seemed too short to make a difference, yet it was easy to repeat. After three weeks, she had practiced on more days than in the previous month. She did not suddenly know every word. However, she felt more confident because studying had become part of her routine. Her teacher asked her to track the days she actually practiced, not the length of her most impressive session. The record made her consistency visible. A small habit that lasts can be more useful than a perfect plan that quickly disappears.

### Day 3 · The Wrong Answer Notebook (143 words · B1)

A student named Minho used to feel disappointed whenever he missed a question. He erased the wrong answer and moved on as quickly as possible. His teacher suggested a different method: next to each mistake, write one sentence explaining why the chosen answer was tempting. At first, Minho thought the extra work was unnecessary. Then he noticed that he often made the same kind of error. He confused the topic of a passage with one interesting detail. When he began to label this mistake, he started looking for ideas repeated across the whole text. He also learned to ask which sentence in the passage supported the correct answer. This made reviewing a mistake feel more like solving a mystery than receiving a punishment. The notebook did not make errors disappear, but it turned them into useful information about what he needed to practice.

### Day 4 · Quiet Time at the Library (125 words · B1)

A library near a busy road received complaints about noise. The manager first considered telling every visitor to remain silent. However, the library also hosted group projects, and students needed places to discuss ideas. The staff decided to divide the building into different zones. One room was reserved for silent reading, while another had tables for quiet conversations. Clear signs explained the purpose of each area. Not everyone was satisfied on the first day, but complaints decreased over time. Students sometimes moved between zones during a single visit. The staff observed that providing choices made it easier for everyone to respect other visitors. The solution worked because it recognized that different activities require different conditions, rather than forcing every visitor to follow a single rule.

### Day 5 · A Helpful Question (132 words · B1)

During a science activity, the teacher asked why a paper boat sank after carrying too many coins. One student answered that the boat was too heavy. The teacher did not simply say yes or no. She asked what would happen if the same coins were placed in a wider boat. Students tested the idea and discovered that the wider boat could hold more coins. The new question helped them realize that the shape of a container can affect how it floats. The teacher encouraged students to explain their guesses before testing them. Some predictions were wrong, but each one gave the class another idea to examine. A useful question does more than check whether someone remembers a fact. It can lead the learner to notice a relationship that was not obvious before.

### Day 1 · A Small Garden Above the Street (117 words · B1)

A school in a crowded city turned part of its flat roof into a garden. At first, the students wanted a pleasant place to eat lunch. Soon, they noticed other changes. The garden also became an outdoor classroom where students measured rainfall and observed how different plants grew. Maintaining it required planning. During school holidays, volunteers had to water the plants and check the soil. The project showed that even a small space can serve several purposes when people design it carefully. It also taught the students that improving a place involves responsibility after the first exciting day.

### Day 1 · The Value of a Failed Experiment (115 words · B2)

When a science experiment fails, students often feel that their time has been wasted. Yet an unexpected result may reveal a weakness in the original idea. Suppose a group builds a paper bridge that collapses under a small weight. Instead of hiding the failure, the students can examine where the bridge bent and compare their design with a stronger one. This process turns the failure into evidence. It is important, however, to record the conditions of the test carefully. If students change several things at once, they may not know which change improved the bridge. A useful experiment is not simply one that succeeds. It is one that helps people understand why a result happened.

**문항 1 — 글의 주장으로 가장 적절한 것은?**

  - A. 실험은 반드시 첫 시도에 성공해야 한다.
  - B. 실험 조건은 결과와 무관하다.
  - **C. 실패도 원인을 이해하는 자료가 될 수 있다.**
  - D. 실패한 실험은 기록할 가치가 없다.
  - **정답**: C
  - **해설**: 실패도 설계의 약점을 드러내며 분석에 쓰일 수 있다고 주장한다.
  - **근거**: `This process turns the failure into evidence.`

**문항 2 — 실험에서 여러 조건을 한꺼번에 바꾸면 생기는 문제는?**

  - A. 실험이 자동으로 성공한다.
  - **B. 어떤 변화가 개선에 영향을 주었는지 알기 어렵다.**
  - C. 자료를 기록할 필요가 없어진다.
  - D. 실험 횟수가 줄어든다.
  - **정답**: B

### Day 2 · The Sentence Inside a Sentence (151 words · B2)

During a reading lesson, students met the sentence, “The scientist who designed the device received an award.” Some thought the subject was the device because it appeared near the verb designed. The teacher drew a line under the main structure: “The scientist received an award.” She then explained that “who designed the device” adds information about the scientist. It does not replace the main verb. The class tried the same technique with a second example containing two relative clauses. By tracing which nouns each clause described, they avoided treating every nearby verb as part of the main structure. The strategy was useful even when students knew the meaning of every individual word. Once students separated the central structure from the extra description, the sentence became easier to understand. Longer English sentences are not always made of difficult words. They can be difficult because familiar words are organized into several connected layers.

### Day 3 · A Map of Ideas (160 words · B2)

A class read an article about school gardens. The article began with the problem of empty playground corners. Next, it described two gardens built by students. Finally, it explained how the gardens changed the way students used the space. Some readers remembered the names of the plants but could not summarize the article. The teacher asked them to draw three boxes labeled problem, action, and result. They filled each box with one sentence, then connected the boxes with arrows. The diagram helped students see why each paragraph was included. After drawing the diagram, students could explain why the article began with a problem instead of a list of plants. They also noticed that the final paragraph showed what changed because of the project. The boxes were not a replacement for reading; they were a way to organize what had been read. Understanding a text often requires more than remembering details; it requires noticing how those details form a meaningful sequence.

### Day 4 · Who Did What? (169 words · B2)

One student reading the article became confused about who had provided the tools. She returned to the sentence, “The mechanic who owned the workshop lent the students a set of tools.” The main verb was lent, and its subject was the mechanic. The phrase “who owned the workshop” identified which mechanic the writer meant. By locating the main verb first, the reader could answer a question that had seemed surprisingly difficult. A different sentence in the article contained a similar pattern, but the word order changed slightly. The teacher asked students to identify the central action before examining descriptions of time, place, and people. When they did this, they were less likely to mistake a nearby noun for the person performing the action. Grammar can be a practical guide to meaning, not merely a set of rules to memorize.

### Day 1 · When Notifications Interrupt Thinking (119 words · B2)

A phone notification often lasts only a second, but its effect may continue after the sound stops. Imagine writing an essay when a message appears. Even if you do not reply, you might wonder who sent it or what it says. Returning to the original sentence then takes another moment of concentration. This does not mean phones are always harmful. They can help students share information, check unfamiliar words, and organize homework. The key is to decide when the device should demand attention. Some students choose a short period with notifications silenced, followed by a planned break to check messages. Such a routine may protect the time needed for difficult thinking without requiring someone to give up useful technology.

A narrow road can become crowded, while a longer road may allow the driver to move steadily. Some customers can receive packages only during a short time window. Others may live in buildings where deliveries must be left at a shared entrance. A useful route plan must therefore consider more than distance. It should balance travel time, delivery promises, and the number of stops a worker can reasonably handle. The goal is not to produce a map that looks simple. It is to create a plan that works reliably for both customers and delivery workers.

Students wondered why the first room felt hotter. They measured the temperature at several times and found that the difference was greatest in the early afternoon. The principal used their results to test a light-colored roof coating on a small section of the building. The school did not assume that one measurement proved everything. It continued to collect data during different kinds of weather. The class also discussed whether the two rooms were otherwise similar. The number of students, open windows, and time of day could all influence the readings. The teacher encouraged them to record these conditions rather than assume that roof color explained the entire difference. This made the project a lesson in comparison as well as heat. The project showed how a familiar discomfort can become a scientific question when observations are recorded and explanations are tested.

### Day 3 · When One Rule Fits Nobody (178 words · B3)

A school introduced the same daily reading target for every student. The rule was easy to explain, but teachers soon noticed problems. Confident readers finished quickly and became bored, while beginners struggled to understand short texts under the time limit. The school changed the program so that all students still practiced regularly but worked with different levels of difficulty. Teachers monitored both comprehension and participation, then adjusted targets as students improved. The revised program was harder to manage because it required more information and judgment. Nevertheless, it better matched the goal of helping each learner make progress. The school also avoided labeling students permanently as strong or weak readers. Teachers reassessed their needs regularly and allowed movement between levels. A student who struggled with science articles might understand historical stories very well, showing that ability could depend partly on the topic. The new system made space for both challenge and support. Flexibility required careful observation instead of simply lowering expectations for some students. A rule can be simple and consistent without being suitable for every person or situation.

### Day 4 · A More Equitable Bus Plan (188 words · B3)

A city evaluated a proposed change to its local bus network. The plan would make popular routes more frequent, improving average waiting time across the city. Yet several neighborhoods with few passengers would lose their only direct connection to a hospital. At a public meeting, planners explained that maximizing the average benefit might overlook residents with limited travel choices. They compared alternative plans using both total passenger time and access to essential services. The final proposal kept some low-demand routes while improving busy corridors. It did not maximize a single numerical measure, but it served a broader public purpose. The planners noticed that the people most affected by a lost route were not always the people who attended public meetings. They consulted health centers and community organizations to learn how residents traveled for essential appointments. Some riders could transfer to another bus, but others faced long walks or additional fares. The city therefore examined the distribution of travel burdens rather than relying on one citywide average to summarize all experiences. Fairness in transport planning requires attention to the people behind the averages, especially those who have fewer alternatives.

### Day 5 · The Last Quiz (215 words · B3)

At the end of a twelve-week English project, a student compared her first diagnostic test with a new assessment. Her vocabulary score had improved, but she still made mistakes when reading passages about unfamiliar topics. She was tempted to memorize more word lists and avoid difficult texts. Her teacher suggested a different response: keep a short record of what each mistake revealed. Some errors came from unknown vocabulary, while others came from missing a contrast or assuming a detail was the main idea. The student planned new practice sessions for each weakness. The final assessment was not a judgment that her learning was finished. It was a tool for deciding what to work on next. During the project, the student had learned that the same wrong answer could result from different causes. A mistake made while guessing after a rushed reading session did not require the same response as a missing grammar concept. Her new notebook separated errors by type and noted a small action for the next session. She could now explain her own improvement more clearly than by reporting a single test score. The process made learning feel like a series of questions she could investigate. Learning becomes more sustainable when evaluation leads to a specific new question rather than a permanent label.
